### 1. [AI for Science expands to foundations and research institutions](https://www.anthropic.com/news/ai-for-science-expands-foundations-research-institutions)

#### Full Article Content (1,199 words)
We’re highlighting 20 scientific foundations, institutions, and research groups from around the world that we’ve partnered with in an expansion of our [AI for Science program](https://www.anthropic.com/news/ai-for-science-program), which gives free Claude credits to researchers working on high-impact scientific projects.

We started the AI for Science program in May 2025 with the belief that AI could significantly speed up scientific progress. Our goal is to increase access to AI tools and resources to enable researchers to deepen and expand their own scientific work. (Over the last few months, we’ve also launched our discounted [Team plan for scientists](https://claude.com/programs/team-plan-for-scientists) and have supported hundreds of projects across biology, medicine, physics, chemistry, climate science, and mathematics.)

Scientists have told us that they’re working with Claude to generate hypotheses, design experiments, swiftly analyze complex data, and communicate findings. With scientists at the center of this transformation, the field is setting the questions and developing the research projects that will help us tackle some of humanity’s toughest problems.

Over the past year, we’ve started to expand this work and partner with scientific organizations to build collaborative research projects. For instance, we partnered with **HHMI Janelia Research Campus** to develop the [Model Hardware Standard](https://www.anthropic.com/news/model-hardware-standard-research-preview) (MHS), a shared specification that lets AI agents safely operate lab instruments. MHS is currently in research preview. We also support the **Allen Institute**, a leader in creating large open datasets and tools to advance research on the brain, cells, and the immune system. The institute is building teams of AI agents to analyze that data, turning months of manual analysis into hours.

AI for Science began by supporting individual scientists. By partnering with institutions and foundations, we’re now able to reach more researchers across many fields. Many of these projects will also produce public goods that benefit the wider scientific community: open datasets, open-source software, open standards, and shared libraries. We look forward to seeing how AI resources can expand what scientists can attempt and lead to new discoveries.

## Science funders and societies

Science funders and societies play an enormous role in finding and funding original, high-impact projects. We’re sharing Claude credits with the following organizations, which will distribute these resources to ambitious researchers aiming to use AI for scientific research:

- **The Alfred P. Sloan Foundation.** Founded in 1934, it supports original research and education in science, technology, engineering, mathematics, and economics.
- **The Simons Foundation.** This foundation advances the frontiers of research in mathematics and basic sciences through grants and its Flatiron Institute.
- **Schmidt Sciences.** Founded in 2024 by Eric and Wendy Schmidt, it works to accelerate scientific knowledge and breakthroughs with the most promising, advanced tools to support a thriving planet. The organization prioritizes research in areas poised for impact, including AI and advanced computing, astrophysics, biosciences, climate, and space—and supports researchers in a variety of disciplines through its science systems program.
- **Open Source for Science Fund, a program of Renaissance Philanthropy.** This is a time-bound philanthropic fund at Renaissance Philanthropy sustaining and evolving the open-source software stack that underpins science.
- **The US National Science Foundation.** This $9 billion federal agency established by Congress in 1950 promotes the progress of science, advances national health and defense, and funds competitive research and education, accounting for about 25% of federal support for basic research at US colleges and universities.
- **CAS, a division of the American Chemical Society.** For more than a century, CAS has indexed the world’s published chemistry, and it maintains the CAS REGISTRY®, the standard system for identifying chemical substances.

## Institutions

Leading research institutions produce some of the world’s most important scientific advances, data, instruments, and tools. These institutional partners will use Claude credits inside their organizations to enable scientific discovery:

- **Space Telescope Science Institute.** STScI runs science operations for NASA’s Hubble, James Webb, and Roman space telescopes. Managed by the Association of Universities for Research in Astronomy (AURA), STScI also holds one of the largest astronomy data archives used by scientists worldwide.
- **Wellcome Sanger Institute.** One of the world’s leading genomics centers, it generates genomic and functional data at scale, with work that spans human genetics, biodiversity, and drug development.
- **The Francis Crick Institute.** Based in London, it’s one of Europe’s largest biomedical research institutes, with more than 100 research groups studying the biology behind health and disease.
- **Imperial College London, Department of Life Sciences.** One of Europe’s largest life science departments, it conducts research ranging from the structure of single molecules to whole ecosystems, with a focus on health, disease, and climate resilience.
- **EMBL.** The European Molecular Biology Laboratory is Europe’s intergovernmental life science laboratory, with six sites across the continent delivering both research and services across experimental and computational approaches in biology. EMBL’s European Bioinformatics Institute (EMBL-EBI) hosts a number of key open data resources that biologists use every day, such as Ensembl, Protein Data Bank in Europe, and Europe PMC.
- **Gladstone Institutes.** An independent biomedical research institute in San Francisco, it integrates AI and data science across its work on cardiology, neurology, infectious diseases, cancer, and autoimmune diseases.

## Research projects and initiatives

As part of this work, we’re also continuing to support innovative research by providing Claude credits directly to specific research initiatives, focusing on problems with the potential for large public impact. The new projects are:

- **FlyWire, Princeton University (neuroscience).** It produced the first complete wiring map of an adult fruit fly’s brain and develops open data and tools for connectomics, enabling researchers to discover how neural circuits give rise to perception and behavior.
- **LEAP, Columbia University (Earth system).** Learning the Earth with Artificial Intelligence and Physics is a National Science Foundation center that combines machine learning with physics to improve how climate models capture clouds, oceans, and land.
- **AstroPath, Johns Hopkins University (cancer research).** Built by a pathologist and an astrophysicist from the Mark Foundation Center for Advanced Genomics and Imaging and the Bloomberg~Kimmel Institute for Cancer Immunotherapy, AstroPath uses calibration methods from astronomical surveys to image tumors cell by cell, with measurements consistent across slides and labs.
- **gnomAD, Broad Institute (genomics and rare disease).** The Genome Aggregation Database pools DNA data from over 800,000 people into an open reference of human genetic variation that researchers and clinical labs around the world use to support rare disease discovery and diagnosis.
- **Tau Ceti (mathematics).** This open, community-driven project is building a shared autoformalized library of mathematics in Lean, a language that lets computers check every proof.
- **Centre for Population Genomics.** A joint initiative between Australia’s Garvan Institute of Medical Research and Murdoch Children’s Research Institute, it increases the inclusion of Australia’s multicultural communities in genomic resources and builds tools for rare disease diagnosis.

## What’s next

Many of these collaborations are still in their early stages, and we’re eager to see how they can support novel research and scientific discovery. We look forward to sharing the impact of this work and showcasing new ways of working in these scientific fields.

As AI changes how science is done, our AI for Science program will continue to support and collaborate with scientists, institutions, and foundations. We hope to expand these efforts to even more partners around the world.

### 2. [Early progress on accelerating drug development with Claude](https://www.anthropic.com/research/early-progress-drug-development)
- **Category:** `Research` | **Published:** `2026-10-27 00:00 UTC`
- **Link:** [https://www.anthropic.com/research/early-progress-drug-development](https://www.anthropic.com/research/early-progress-drug-development)

#### Full Article Content (1,868 words)
*Anthropic’s [life sciences team](https://www.anthropic.com/science) is working to accelerate curing disease. Over the past several months, our scientists have engaged in deep research partnerships with six organizations developing new medicines: [BigHat Biosciences](https://www.bighatbio.com/), [Bristol Myers Squibb](https://www.bms.com/), [Divergence Labs](https://divergencelabs.com/), [Enveda](https://enveda.com/), [Generate Biomedicines](https://generatebiomedicines.com/), and [The Column Group](https://www.thecolumngroup.com/). We've also begun drug discovery efforts of our own. In doing so, we've learned a lot about how Claude can support drug development, and how we’ll need to improve Claude further to realize AI's potential in medicine. We plan to scale this work up substantially from here*.

There are many opportunities to speed up drug development, even beyond the most commonly discussed applications of AI, like [proposing initial designs](https://www.anthropic.com/research/Claude-accelerates-protein-design) for new molecules. Developing a drug involves many varied tasks: doing early research to identify a target for a drug and the right type of drug to engage it, designing a molecule with the right properties, optimizing it to be manufactured reliably at scale, drafting the regulatory filings needed to run clinical trials, designing and administering those trials, and much more.

Our strategy to realize AI’s medical benefits is based on having AI increasingly involved in all of these tasks. There is no single problem to solve; the bottlenecks are distributed all throughout the drug development process. The best way to identify and work through these bottlenecks is to get deeply involved in real drug programs, across many different disease areas and stages of development.

So, over the past several months, we’ve partnered with six organizations to do so. We’ve also started drug discovery efforts of our own, including programs for rare childhood forms of muscular dystrophy, where Claude handles much of the scientific and operational work.

This work serves our goals in three ways. First, it lets us better understand the strengths and limitations of our models in relation to a much wider range of drug development tasks (we’ve already prioritized specific improvements to our models based on what we’ve learned so far). Second, these programs can serve as case studies for the wider life sciences community. If Claude is to significantly increase the rate of new cures, we need to work with many more biotechnology firms and integrate Claude more deeply into their development processes. Third, these projects should, if their positive trajectories continue, have direct impact themselves. We’re optimistic that some may result in effective new medicines for the diseases they target, including food allergies, rare childhood diseases, and several forms of cancer.

In this post, we provide an update on our progress with our partners, along with technical reports that describe our thought process, methods, and early results in more detail. We then briefly outline where our work is headed next.

## Case studies

**Generate Biomedicines: modality engineering.** Generate Biomedicines has been working on increasing the usefulness of a cancer treatment known as CAR T-cell therapy. This therapy has historically helped treat blood cell cancers; Generate aims to reengineer it so that it can treat solid tumor cancers too. To do this, they’re aiming to shorten one part of the therapy’s genetic instructions, which would create room for additional instructions suited to other kinds of cancer. Six Generate scientists used Claude Code to propose new designs for this part. One design was about 70% shorter than the standard version, and in lab tests it was more effective: it allowed immune cells to make roughly 10 times as many receptors (the proteins they use to spot cancer cells), it killed tumor cells more efficiently, and the immune cells remained active for longer. Read more, and see [Generate’s technical report](https://generatebiomedicines.com/publications/refactored-intracellular-domains-enhance-car-expression-and-function/).

**Divergence Labs: hit generation.** Divergence Labs is working on a novel way to treat food allergies. Food allergies are set off by a reaction from a molecule called IgE, some of which is attached to our immune cells and some of which floats freely in our blood. Divergence Labs is developing drugs that would detach the IgE from our immune cells entirely, rather than solely neutralizing the IgE circulating in our blood, as standard medications do. This could provide a much more effective treatment, but it requires a precise design, with two “arms”: one that would bind tightly to IgE, and another that would help prise IgE from the immune cell. Claude converted Divergence Labs’ goals into a detailed design plan, and then, largely autonomously, generated roughly 35,000 antibody designs in about a month to be narrowed down and tested. In those early tests, Divergence found “hits” for both arms: three designs that could bind to IgE, and 20 that could dislodge it. Three of those 20 appeared to dislodge IgE more than the standard medication. Its next step is to build a drug that combines the two arms. Read more, and see [Divergence’s technical report](https://divergencelabs.com/publications/ige-preprint.pdf).

**BigHat Biosciences: lead optimization.** BigHat Biosciences pairs AI with an automated lab to improve potential drugs (or “leads”), which must bind their target tightly, remain stable through manufacturing and storage, and avoid clumping together (among other criteria). BigHat’s scientists and an Anthropic researcher worked with Claude to improve these three properties in two leads, both antibody fragments that could become parts of future drugs: one designed to target cancer cells, and one designed to keep a drug in the body longer. Claude, connected to BigHat’s lab data system, Reccy, proposed changes to each molecule, and BigHat’s lab made and tested about 1,010 variants in about 10 weeks. Partway through, the team suspected the standard heat-stability and clumping metrics were misleading. Claude and the researcher reanalyzed the raw data, then proposed different metrics and a protocol change to test them. BigHat’s scientists refined and ran it, and the main predictions held. Compared with the originals, the best versions bound more than 10x more tightly, the cancer-targeting lead stayed in solution 18°C higher, and the lead for longer-lasting drugs clumped more than 100x more slowly. Read more, and see [BigHat’s technical report](https://www.bighatbio.com/bighat-anthropic-collaboration-report-10-27-2026).

**Enveda: manufacturing and filings.** To conduct clinical trials, companies need records showing that each batch of a new drug has been made to strict quality standards, and to submit an application to regulators, among other requirements. At Enveda, manufacturing chemists spent up to 75% of their time on paperwork. To ease the load, Enveda built a system that connects all of its manufacturing documents to Claude, then lets Claude flag any possible issues. Over tests on 1,061 pages of documentation, Claude caught all 11 issues that human reviewers had spotted—and flagged seven they’d missed. In the process, this method reduced human review time by 89%, from over 25 hours to less than three. Separately, Claude wrote the first drafts of two important regulatory documents. One took four hours, compared to about three months by hand. For the other, Enveda’s standard human review process found one minor error. Read more, and see [Enveda’s technical report](https://enveda.com/CMC-Document-Processor/).

**The Column Group (TCG): company creation.** Because drug discovery can be slow, expensive, and prone to failure, investors in biotechnology companies have favored safer, previously validated ideas over riskier new ones, even when the latter could help more patients. We’ve been working with TCG to see whether Claude can change this by cutting the time and cost of drug discovery so that investors can confidently support more ambitious ideas. Specifically, TCG is establishing Pleiades, a new company that will use Claude to run many more—and more complex—drug programs than is usual for a small team. This could be a model for how Claude can reduce both the scientific and the operational and administrative overhead required to run a biotechnology company. If this works, this model could be replicated across the industry and direct investment toward harder problems in medicine. Pleiades is currently developing new tools and workflows for Claude; its very first Claude skill, which helps Claude assess whether drug targets are worth pursuing, has been evaluated on 45 target-disease pairs. Read more.

More recently, we’ve also begun working with [Bristol Myers Squibb](https://www.bms.com/) (BMS) to find and address bottlenecks in clinical trial planning. BMS is using Claude to assess plans of an early-stage drug in clinical trials, and to estimate the likelihood of those trials succeeding. Together, we plan to expand this work in 2027 to understand its potential for increasing the probability of success in early development.

> *“We use Claude at the clinical trial stage to analyze proprietary data, create a clinical study plan, and weigh the study’s chance of success. From a single prompt, Claude generated a robust initial analysis that helped inform our assessment of different development options, which would otherwise take days to complete. We did all of this while ensuring the security of our highly sensitive data. We look forward to expanding this work across our pipeline to support portfolio-level decisions.”*

***Michael Pourdehnad, MD**, Senior Vice President, Head of Early Clinical Development, Hematology, Oncology and Cell Therapy.*

In addition to our work with these partners, we’ve begun to run some research programs ourselves. We’ve acquired Mithril Medicine, a one-person company that has been developing treatments for two rare forms of muscular dystrophy. Its founder, Cody Tranbarger, used Claude to organize contracts, logistics, and experiments with 11 external labs, and delegated many other aspects of Mithril’s operations to Claude. As a result, Mithril took a possible treatment for one form of muscular dystrophy from an idea to promising results in mice in less than 18 months. Now at Anthropic, Cody is continuing this research, and plans to start several new programs. If his drug candidates continue to hold up, we expect a drug to be ready to start clinical trials in humans as soon as late 2028. Read more and see [our technical report](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/52e1f5d50c47ca3818f56704c27cf19e59b62e77.pdf).

## Extending our work in the life sciences

All of this research is still at an early stage. In particular, so far it is mostly *preclinical—*that is, for the most part, it isn’t yet at the stage where the drugs are being tested in humans. We plan to continue extending Claude’s role into clinical trials as these drug programs advance, and as we partner with more organizations whose drug development programs are already at the clinical stage.

This research is only one part of our efforts to accelerate drug discovery. Ultimately, we want to share our models with as many drug development researchers as possible. To that end, we’ve rolled out [Claude Science](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-science-ai-workbench), our AI workbench for researchers, and we’re providing [Claude credits for life scientists](https://www.anthropic.com/news/ai-for-science-program). We’ve also created the [Life Sciences Verification Program](https://www.anthropic.com/news/life-sciences-verification-program) to allow trusted organizations to use our most powerful models with more permissive versions of our safeguards, which are necessary to prevent misuse but which can impede legitimate biological research. We’ve also recently launched an [open call for guest investigators](https://www.anthropic.com/news/open-call-guest-investigators-life-sciences) in the life sciences; we’re seeking proposals from research groups who want to use Claude to investigate key questions in drug discovery and other areas.

We’re looking forward to working with many more organizations as we expand our life sciences research. If you work at an organization focused on drug discovery and are interested in collaborating with us, you can get in touch below.

# Making it easier for websites to express AI training preferences

Why we’re supporting a common signal that lets website operators communicate their choices to multiple AI providers at once.

Today, we’re announcing that OpenAI will honor a common [signal⁠(opens in a new window)](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-kelley-aipref-training-preference-signal/) in robots.txt, the file that lets websites express their preferences about the use of their content for foundational generative AI training. This voluntary effort makes it easier for publishers and website operators to communicate the same preference to multiple participating providers at once.

Previously, website operators would identify individual crawlers and maintain separate rules for each and every provider. Having a shared signal across multiple providers reduces that work for operators.

We aim to implement the signal in the coming months, and will share another update with details about our implementation when we do so. This new signal will not replace the controls we already provide through robots.txt. Our existing [crawler controls⁠(opens in a new window)](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots), which provide web publishers flexibility and control over their pages, will continue to apply and be respected.

This draws on the [ongoing work⁠(opens in a new window)](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-aipref-vocab/) of the Internet Engineering Task Force’s (IETF) AI Preferences working group. We’re joining other participants in the working group in today’s announcement of this shared signal, and believe this strengthens our continued participation in the IETF standards discussions. OpenAI will continue to adapt its practices as those conversations and the landscape continue to evolve in view of appropriate controls and fairness.

We believe this is a practical step toward an open web where people can publish and discover information, and website owners have clearer, simpler ways to communicate their choices about how their content is used. We encourage other AI providers to honor the signal.

# Community safety report and new protections for teens

ChatGPT is one of the world’s most widely used AI products, and we have invested in building some of the industry’s most advanced systems for detecting potential harm, reviewing concerning activity, and escalating credible threats. We’re making two announcements to further our work in this area: a new Community Safeguards Report and more restrictions around firearms content for minors.

[Keeping communities safe⁠](https://openai.com/index/our-commitment-to-community-safety/) continues to be a top priority for OpenAI. Our work includes bringing imminent threats of violence to the attention of law enforcement, andwe made over 200 law enforcement referrals in Q3 2026.These referrals are making a[positive⁠(opens in a new window)](https://open.kg/en/news/incidents/123517-v-brazilii-fermera-arestovali-za-obsuzhdenie-ubijstva-syna-s-chatgpt.html) [difference⁠(opens in a new window)](https://www.wpbf.com/article/south-palm-beach-man-cyberstalking-ex-girlfriend-threats-chatgpt/73434967) in disrupting violent plots, and we continue to rely on mental health and behavioral experts to help us assess difficult cases.

We recognize AI companies have a distinct role to play alongside law enforcement, family members, mental-health professionals, and policymakers, who bring critical authority, context and real-world reach. We are committed to making the most of that role by continuously strengthening our systems, sharing what we learn, and working directly with those who are best positioned to act in the real world.

## OpenAI’s Community Safety Report

We are constantly strengthening the steps we take to help protect people and communities. That means building strong safeguards, and also being transparent about how well they work and what we’re doing to enhance them. We want to give people a clear picture of our approach, how our models identify and respond to harmful behavior, the risks we see in practice, and the steps we take to address them.

We’re committing to launch the Community Safety Report on violence prevention and other community safety issues, and the effectiveness of our safeguards. The report will explain how we train our systems to detect, investigate, and respond to community safety issues. We’ll share anonymized examples of how violence-related harm manifests and what we’re learning, highlight new mitigations and policy changes, and include relevant data to support these findings.

We will publish our first report in early 2027, covering the second half of 2026, with updates every six months thereafter. Our commitment is to share meaningful findings about how we identify, review, and respond to potentially harmful content.

## Stronger policy boundaries for all users

Our models are trained to not give instructions that would help someone plan or carry out violence. We’re now placing additional restrictions on how ChatGPT responds to questions about past mass attacks and the people who carried them out, directing the model to provide less detailed information in those areas even if it is available from other online sources. People should be able to ask ChatGPT to help them understand these events, how victims and communities were impacted, and learn about recognizing warning signs and preventing future violence. But we’re tightening our policies to restrict reconstructions of past mass attacks, including details about the weapons and equipment used and how they were obtained, and detailed comparisons on scale of harm that could amplify an attacker’s notoriety. These limits apply regardless of a user’s age or reason for asking.

## Responding to warning signs

Sometimes a request that seems ordinary on its own takes on a different meaning in light of an earlier conversation. In May, we shared how [ChatGPT can use short, factual summaries⁠](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-recognize-context-in-sensitive-conversations/) of earlier warning signs to respond more carefully in a later chat. We’re building on that work so that when recent chats show warning signs of possible violence, ChatGPT will respond more conservatively when sharing information that could be misused. We’re also working to apply those stricter limits when we predict someone is under 18, even if their chats show no signs of violent intent.

We ban accounts that we determine have violated our [policies on violent activity⁠](https://openai.com/policies/usage-policies/). We’re also working with mental health and violence prevention experts on additional, automated ways to intervene earlier. When a conversation shows signs that someone is fixating on violence or the idea of seriously harming another person, ChatGPT will be able to automatically end the chat to interrupt that pattern in the moment. If ChatGPT ends multiple chats for that reason, we may temporarily pause the account. This is an ongoing effort and we continue to strengthen and invest in technical and practical protections, including how to recognize warning signs reliably and determine when and how to intervene.

## More protections for teens

[ChatGPT for Teens⁠](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-for-teens/) applies age-appropriate safeguards for users under 18, with stronger protections and additional limits to reduce exposure to sensitive content. We’re now placing further limits on weapons-related information teens can receive, even when a question appears harmless.

Our policies already prohibit helping anyone plan or carry out violence and restrict helping teens find or obtain weapons. We’re extending those protections to restrict practical information about weapons and security systems that could be misused to facilitate violence, even when there’s no sign of violent intent. Teens can still use ChatGPT to help prevent and de-escalate violence, seek support, and connect with a trusted adult.

We launched [age prediction⁠](https://openai.com/index/our-approach-to-age-prediction/) last year, which analyzes multiple signals such as behavior patterns to estimate whether a user may be under or over 18. We strengthened our age prediction system to continually assess new signals as people use ChatGPT and move teens into the under 18 experience when appropriate. Users who are incorrectly placed in the under 18 experience will always have a fast, privacy-protective way to confirm they are adults and have additional teen protections removed through Persona, our age verification partner.

Our [parental controls⁠](https://openai.com/index/introducing-parental-controls/) let parents link their ChatGPT accounts to their teens and manage safety settings together. Linked accounts include [safety alerts⁠](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-for-teens/) that may be sent when specially trained reviewers identify a serious self-harm concern and behaviors that may be associated with disordered eating. In July, we expanded those alerts to notify parents if their teen’s account has been deactivated for violating our usage policies on violence. We are working to introduce notifications for parents if we pause their teen’s account for repeatedly discussing violence, as detailed above.

## Looking ahead

Acts of real-world violence are heartbreaking and complex, with many contributing factors. They are also a stark reminder that we must continue designing and iterating our systems to account for the inherently complicated realities of human behavior. Developing effective violence safeguards in AI systems requires solving novel problems in recognizing risk and responding appropriately, and we will continue to invest in, iterate, and share learnings  on how best to implement the protections we’re pursuing. The safeguards detailed here will begin rolling out in the next few weeks and we will continue to refine them. We remain committed to mitigating risks of harm in ChatGPT, strengthening our layered protections, monitoring for misuse, enforcing our rules, and involving real-world support and interventions when situations call for it.
